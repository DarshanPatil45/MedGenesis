## What is diabetes?

Diabetes is a chronic health condition that affects how the body turns food
into energy. The body breaks down most food into sugar (glucose) and releases
it into the bloodstream. When blood sugar rises, the pancreas releases
insulin, which acts as a key to let blood sugar into cells for use as energy.
With diabetes, the body either does not make enough insulin or cannot use the
insulin it makes as well as it should. Too much blood sugar stays in the
bloodstream over time, which can cause serious health problems such as heart
disease, vision loss, and kidney disease.

## Type 2 diabetes

With type 2 diabetes, the body does not use insulin well and cannot keep
blood sugar at normal levels. It develops over many years and is usually
diagnosed in adults, though it is increasingly diagnosed in children and
teens. Symptoms may develop slowly and may not be noticeable, so blood sugar
testing is important for people at risk.

## Prediabetes

Prediabetes means blood sugar levels are higher than normal but not high
enough yet to be classified as type 2 diabetes. Prediabetes raises the risk
of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, and stroke. Many people with prediabetes
do not know they have it. Lifestyle change programs have been shown to help
people with prediabetes lower their risk of developing type 2 diabetes.

## Risk factors for type 2 diabetes

Risk of developing type 2 diabetes is higher in people who have prediabetes,
are overweight, are 45 years or older, have a parent or sibling with type 2
diabetes, are physically active less than three times a week, or have ever
had gestational diabetes (diabetes during pregnancy) or given birth to a baby
who weighed over nine pounds. Race and ethnicity are also factors: African
American, Hispanic/Latino, American Indian, Alaska Native, Pacific Islander,
and some Asian American people are at higher risk.

## Physical activity

Regular physical activity helps the body use insulin more effectively and can
lower blood sugar. General guidance for adults is at least 150 minutes per
week of moderate-intensity activity, such as brisk walking, spread across the
week, plus muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days per week. People
who have been inactive should start slowly and build up gradually. Even short
sessions of activity add up across a day.

## Healthy eating

Eating patterns that support blood sugar management emphasize non-starchy
vegetables, whole fruits, whole grains, lean proteins, and water in place of
sugar-sweetened drinks. Portion awareness and consistent meal timing help
keep blood sugar steadier. There is no single required diet; the best eating
plan is one that fits a person's preferences, culture, and health goals and
can be sustained over time.

## Weight management

For people who are overweight, losing a modest amount of body weight — often
described as about 5 to 7 percent of starting weight — combined with regular
physical activity has been shown in prevention programs to substantially
reduce the risk of developing type 2 diabetes. Gradual, sustained change is
more effective than rapid weight loss that is not maintained.

## Blood sugar monitoring

Monitoring helps people understand how food, activity, medicine, stress, and
illness affect their blood sugar. How often to check, and what target range
to aim for, depends on the individual and should be decided with a health
care team. The A1C test reflects average blood sugar over the past two to
three months and is commonly used to diagnose and monitor diabetes.

## Working with a health care team

Diabetes care is managed with a health care team that may include a primary
care provider, a diabetes care and education specialist, a registered
dietitian, and others. Regular check-ups allow monitoring for complications
affecting the eyes, feet, kidneys, and heart. Any decision about medication,
testing frequency, or treatment targets should be made with a qualified
clinician.

## Smoking and alcohol

People with diabetes who smoke are more likely to have serious health
problems, including heart and kidney disease. Quitting smoking lowers these
risks. If alcohol is consumed, doing so in moderation and with awareness of
its effect on blood sugar is advised.

## Sleep and stress

Not getting enough sleep can make it harder to manage blood sugar and can
increase appetite and weight gain. Stress can also raise blood sugar levels.
Regular sleep schedules and stress-management approaches such as physical
activity, relaxation techniques, and social support are part of overall
diabetes care.
